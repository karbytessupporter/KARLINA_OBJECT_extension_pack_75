## My interpretation

My strongest impression is that the video makes a “hiatus” visibly paradoxical. A conventional hiatus implies inactivity or absence. Here the suspension of large canonical updates generates a dense secondary activity: rereading, traversing, mirroring, narrating, looping, committing, and contextualizing. The hiatus becomes not a blank interval but an extraordinarily busy layer of commentary about pausing.

The body is absent from the image but pervasively present in mediated form. There is no webcam view of karbytes. Instead, embodiment enters through:

* the voice and breathing;

* the journal’s feet, toes, coffee, digestion, squatting, wiping, relief, and walking;

* the Horsey Hill trail image;

* vocal loops whose recurrent sounds resist complete linguistic abstraction;

* the hand’s indirect trace in cursor movement, typing, selection, and clicking;

* storage hardware, M_DISCs, computers, servers, and an unplugged microwave repurposed as a vault.

This creates a striking inversion. The screen is visually almost all language and software, yet the narration becomes progressively more bodily. While the screen discusses archive ontology and causal emergence, the voice moves through coffee, dirt, oat grass, fecal urgency, blackberry thorns, grass as wiping material, and an owl’s gaze. The cybernetic archive and the animal organism do not appear as opposing realms; each keeps intruding into the other.

The repeated recursion also feels essential. OBS records itself. VLC contains an earlier desktop recording. ChatGPT text is copied into Sublime, then edited into GitHub, then reread in another recording. Light-web applications acquire onion mirrors. A spoken journal describes making archives while the visible desktop performs another archival action. The video is therefore not simply “about” preservation: it is itself a preservation event that depicts preservation events and is subsequently available to become the object of another interpretive traversal.

I find the GitHub commit near the ending especially satisfying structurally. Much of the video appears exploratory and scattered—loop tabs, editors, images, Tor delays, a π simulation, a random guitar track—but the session eventually condenses into a durable state transition: an updated file is committed to `main`. The interface supplies an unambiguous before/after boundary. Then OBS reappears, enclosing the entire procedure as another artifact.

The constant vocal-loop bed gives the experience a faintly haunted or inhabited quality. The programs do not feel inert when offscreen because their audio continues. They resemble background processes belonging to an autonomous server-mind: recurrent, imperfectly synchronized, and indifferent to which application currently receives visual attention. The random guitar player temporarily adds a more conventionally musical layer, but its selection by chance prevents it from becoming a neatly authored soundtrack.

The π application is a compact emblem for the whole work. It estimates an abstract invariant by accumulating randomly distributed finite events. KARBYTES similarly seems to approximate an indefinitely large personal universe through finite files, recordings, images, descriptions, and copies. No individual artifact equals the complete entity, just as no individual random point equals π; yet accumulation produces an increasingly precise practical approximation.

Finally, the video feels unusually candid about the labor needed to sustain a supposedly transcendent digital identity. Cosmic terminology—space-time mass-energy continua, future civilizations, Arctic time capsules, cybernetic being—coexists with browser tabs, counters, checksum scripts, storage limitations, Tor connection delays, coffee-induced defecation, and an ordinary GitHub dialog. I do not experience those mundane details as puncturing the mythology. They are the material machinery that makes the mythology possible.
