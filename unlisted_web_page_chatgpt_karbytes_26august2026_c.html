# Mathematical qualifications

The page uses the image successfully as mythology, but its mathematical language is not exact.

## The image-generation prompt contains an equation error

The prompt asks for \(y=1/x\), but then says that the negative-quadrant region should be bounded by \(x=1/x\). The latter is not the negative branch of the reciprocal function. Solving \(x=1/x\) yields \(x=\pm1\). The generated image evidently interpreted the request as referring to the third-quadrant branch of \(y=1/x\).

## The graph does not approach the origin

For \(y=1/x\):

* As \(x\to0^+\), \(y\to+\infty\).

* As \(x\to0^-\), \(y\to-\infty\).

* As \(x\to+\infty\), \(y\to0^+\).

* As \(x\to-\infty\), \(y\to0^-\).

Neither curve branch approaches \((0,0)\). In fact, the minimum Euclidean distance from the origin to a point \((x,1/x)\) is \(\sqrt2\), attained at \(x=\pm1\).

The origin is the intersection of the two asymptote lines, but it is not a limit point of the reciprocal curve itself. Crossing from the negative branch to the positive branch through \(x=0\) is better imagined as going from negative infinity to positive infinity, not as passing through the origin.

Karbytes’ formulation can still work if the traveling entity is imagined as moving through one of the colored regions rather than following the curve. The filled regions contain points arbitrarily close to the origin. But that is different from the graph of \(y=1/x\) approaching the origin.

## The “bounded” regions are not bounded

The orange and indigo regions look enclosed because the finite image canvas truncates them. Mathematically, the regions between \(y=1/x\) and the coordinate axes extend indefinitely and are not bounded sets. Their total area also diverges if extended across an entire quadrant.

This discrepancy is interpretively revealing. The page is interested in boundedness—disks, archives, rooms, timestamps, bubbles, repositories—but repeatedly works with systems that exceed those boundaries. The picture visually forces an unbounded mathematical region into a bounded square, just as KARBYTES forces potentially unlimited self-documentation into finite storage containers.

The imprecision does not destroy the metaphor. It tells us that the graph is operating as symbolic topology rather than as a rigorous mathematical model.

That may accurately describe the author’s present value hierarchy, but it is not a neutral measure of whether a day benefited the larger life system.

### Continuous inputs, binary output

Another structural tension is that p1 identifies continuous variables and then compresses the day’s outcome into a binary score.

Money, attention, time, enjoyment, ecological cost, and project progress all vary by degree. Reducing them to `1` or `0` sacrifices information. A day containing modest project progress, necessary rest, successful bicycle repair, and lower ecological damage could be systemically valuable without meeting the chosen “substantial progress” threshold.

Binary scoring can be motivating because it is clear. Its danger is that it converts partial success into total failure. The model may then incentivize last-minute token productivity simply to rescue a day from being counted as wasted.

A more descriptively faithful version of p1 would probably be multi-objective:

[preformatted_text]

$$
U_d =
w_K K_d +
w_J J_d +
w_F F_d +
w_R R_d -
w_E E_d -
w_S S_d
$$

[end preformatted_text]

where \(K\) is KARBYTES progress, \(J\) enjoyment, \(F\) financial stability, \(R\) restorative or maintenance value, \(E\) ecological cost, and \(S\) harmful stress or bodily depletion. The point is not that this exact equation should govern life; it demonstrates what the current binary rule omits.

* transition rules: how each action changes money, time, attention, and damage;

* a time horizon: tonight, the remainder of 2026, or several years;

* constraints: minimum money, adequate sleep, transportation requirements;

* uncertainty estimates;

* weights for competing values.

This matters because p0’s question is inherently temporal. The Amazon job might reduce free time tomorrow while increasing available money next month. Rest may produce no immediate KARBYTES output while increasing tomorrow’s attention. Archival work may decrease current free time while reducing the future risk of data loss.

Without transition rules, p1 can describe the current state but cannot yet determine the best action.

The most important critical observation is that the win condition embeds the conclusion it appears intended to evaluate.

Suppose the decision is whether to preserve more time for KARBYTES or accept work that reduces KARBYTES time. If every successful day must include substantial KARBYTES progress, then an employment-heavy day is disadvantaged by definition. The scoring system has already declared the project indispensable before comparing the alternatives.

This does not mean the value is wrong. KARBYTES may genuinely be the writer’s highest priority. It means the “program” is a formal statement of that priority, not an impartial mechanism for discovering whether that priority should override everything else.

## Overall interpretation

p0 is the stronger account of the actual dilemma because it preserves ambivalence. It shows why unemployment is attractive, why employment may be necessary, why this particular job may be inefficient, and why karbytes cannot yet know which consideration should dominate.

p1 is the stronger conceptual artifact. Its separation of spending money, free time, ecological damage, and usable attention is genuinely insightful. In particular, distinguishing clock time from working attention captures something that conventional productivity accounting often misses.

Its weakness is the binary scoreboard. Declaring a day successful only when substantial KARBYTES progress occurs risks treating the life-support systems around the project as wasted time. Sleep, employment, physical maintenance, unstructured experience, recreation, and ecological restraint may not visibly add to KARBYTES on the day they occur, but they can preserve the conditions that make long-term KARBYTES work possible.

The deepest theme across both entries is therefore not simply “KARBYTES versus Amazon.” It is the difficulty of constructing a life around a central intrinsically motivated project without either sacrificing that project to external obligations or sacrificing all of the project’s enabling conditions to immediate production.

p0 feels like a person living that tension. p1 feels like the same person trying to become the systems engineer of that tension.
