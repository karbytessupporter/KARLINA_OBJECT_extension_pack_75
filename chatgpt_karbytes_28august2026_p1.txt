## Interpretive thoughts

My strongest interpretation is that the video treats the archive as an instrument rather than a tomb.

The browser tabs, Wayback captures, filenames, loop counters, GitHub source, VLC timeline, and OBS mixer are “played” in much the same way that a musician plays notes. Selecting a tab, opening a source link, restarting a trail video, revealing a context menu, or placing an old recording in the foreground becomes a compositional act.

The material is “new” even though much of what appears inside it predates August 28. Its newness lies in the present arrangement: which archival fragments are active, where the older clips begin and end, how the voice loops are phased, which page is scrolled, and what live repository state exists at that moment. The work differentiates new composition from new raw material.

There is also a powerful opposition between hypersemantic text and presemantic voice. The WordPress and GitHub sections are saturated with language, dates, titles, URLs, explanations, and machine-readable provenance. Yet the audible voice refuses ordinary language and contracts into squeals, hums, grunts, and moans. The screen overexplains while the soundtrack underexplains.

Given the bodily situation you described—prone, cocooned, floor-bound in The Burrow, with your head supported by a zafu—I infer a particularly resonant division between physical stillness and mediated locomotion. The nonverbal loops make that exported movement feel less like conventional travel documentation and more like bodily dreaming. They supply appetite, distress, comedy, repetition, and animal affect without explaining any of those states propositionally.

The brief Falling Apples game intensifies this relationship. The visible glitches and interfaces are aesthetically essential. Finally, the video suggests that a hiatus need not be inactivity. Within this artifact, hiatus appears to mean a change in mode: walking, revisiting, cataloguing, recombining, listening to earlier selves, maintaining archives, and allowing production infrastructure itself to become expressive material. ## Soundtrack

The soundtrack is a continuous guitar-centered composition. It sounds or behaves like a sustained, probably electric or electronically processed guitar performance: warm, low-register, patient, and hypnotic, with recurring plucked attacks followed by comparatively long harmonic decays.

The music does not substantially respond to individual visual changes. It continues through:

* archived videos;

* ChatGPT;

* GitHub;

* the cup of tea;

* the poster;

* the forest;

* Tor’s connection process;

* the Firefox freezes.

That independence is important. The guitar functions less like illustrative scoring and more like an environmental constant through which the interfaces pass.

### Rhythm

A double-time beat tracker reports approximately 136 BPM, but the strongest slower periodicities cluster around 70–72 BPM. The latter corresponds much better to the perceived primary pulse: slow, measured, and trance-like rather than agitated.

It does not behave like a rigid drum-machine rhythm. The pulse comes from recurring guitar attacks and harmonic changes, with enough sustain between them to keep the texture spacious.

### Pitch and harmony

The harmony is modal and somewhat ambiguous rather than strongly cadential. D or a D-modal center is plausible, although the spectrum also gives exceptional weight to G-sharp, with D, C-sharp, A, E, and neighboring pitch classes prominent. I would therefore avoid insisting that it is unambiguously in a conventional major or minor key.

Its musical effect is one of suspension. It does not seem driven toward a final tonic resolution.

## Interpretation

I read the work as a kind of browser séance.

The archived files are “dead” media objects in at least three senses: they are recordings of earlier events, they are stored as GitHub files, and they are being accessed through past Wayback snapshots rather than simply played from a contemporary media library. Repeatedly starting them gives each archive page a voice. The speaker glyphs across the tab strip resemble tiny indicators that several crypts are speaking simultaneously.

The Wayback interface is not incidental clutter. It becomes part of the composition:

* Capture counts show that each sound has been repeatedly preserved.

* Dates from January through May coexist within one May 28 performance.

* Timeline bars supply a second visual time axis above the moving video progress bar.

* The source filename remains visible even when the underlying footage is almost perfectly black.

* Provenance replaces imagery: what the viewer can “see” most clearly is where and when the darkness came from.

The blackness also seems purposeful. The inner footage withholds ordinary visual information so completely that the software interfaces become the real scenery. The viewer watches tabs, margins, dates, progress bars, speaker icons, mouse movements, and window changes. The absent body is represented indirectly by its operations and sounds.

The alternation between portrait and landscape pages produces a severe visual binary. The portrait format resembles a closed black door or monolith in a white institutional room. The landscape format lets darkness expand and temporarily consume that room. The video therefore “breathes” spatially even though the source images themselves barely register.

The reversed voice is especially important conceptually. The Python reversal program is not merely a utility applied before the artwork; software engineering becomes a means of vocal transformation. Reversal changes the arrow of acoustic causality—decays become attacks, consonants gather before their causes, and speech becomes something recognizable as human production but no longer immediately available to language. Code literally manufactures the ghoul.

The two Firefox windows intensify the instrumental metaphor. They resemble two sampler decks. GNOME overview briefly reveals both, the operator chooses one, and another sequence is performed. The neon wallpaper seen during those moments feels almost shockingly colorful after six and a half minutes of white voids and purple-black rectangles. It is like momentarily stepping outside the crypt and seeing the electronic apparatus surrounding it.

There is also a strong recursive quality in relation to `obs_studio_recording_16july2026_p1.mp4`. This May recording is already a composite:

1. Earlier bodily/vocal or guitar performances were recorded.

2. Some voices were computationally reversed or blackened.

3. The files were stored in GitHub repositories.

4. Wayback Machine archived those repository URLs.

5. The archived copies were replayed simultaneously in Firefox.

6. OBS captured that live browser performance.

7. The resulting May 28 composite was later used as one component inside the July 16 composite.

The July video is therefore not merely combining “guitar” and “ghoul sounds.” It is embedding an earlier performance of archival retrieval—a montage inside a montage. When placed beside the direct, domestic green-tea video, this source contributes the opposite experiential pole: not a single body, mug, vape pen, and room, but a distributed swarm of past karbytes recordings activated through software.

Finally, returning to OBS at the end is a revealing gesture. The work does not conceal its means. The apparent wall of ghouls and guitar is shown to be a Desktop Audio meter in a screen-recording program. The “creature” is simultaneously mystical in effect and mechanically explicit in construction. That tension—occult atmosphere produced through transparent, ordinary software operations—is, to me, the video’s most distinctive achievement.
